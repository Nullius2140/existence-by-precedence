Where the institutional reader pushes back
1. The category error is the remedy problem. The essay defines a constitution as rules that bind the strong and celebrates that no balance has ever been edited "however sympathetic the case." An institutional reader states the inversion plainly: a system with no mechanism to reverse theft, fraud, or duress is not a constitution; it is a constitution with the entire judiciary deleted and the word "judiciary" reassigned to signature-checking. The essay's "judges" adjudicate nothing — no facts, no intent, no proportionality, no remedy. What law calls justice is precisely the capacity to edit entitlements for cause, under procedure. The essay treats that capacity as the corruption; law treats it as the point. This is not a misunderstanding to be argued away — it is a genuine values conflict, and the essay would be stronger if it named it as such instead of annexing the vocabulary of the courtroom.

2. The record's own history is softer than claimed. "Not one balance has been edited" survives scrutiny only under a careful definition. In August 2010, the value-overflow incident created 184 billion coins; the chain was reorganized and those entries erased. Defensible — the coins were invalid under the rules — but then "balances may not be edited" quietly becomes "balances we retroactively deem valid may not be edited," and the 2013 fork reorg reversed confirmed transactions as collateral. A committee's fact-checker finds this in an afternoon. The claim that survives is weaker but still remarkable: no valid balance has been edited by majority decision. The essay should say the weaker true thing.

3. The veil-of-ignorance argument convicts the first essay. This is the sharpest internal contradiction in the trilogy. Part II says: where a faction knew it would hold power and drafted accordingly, the document was "a charter of privilege wearing a constitution's clothes." Now apply that test to the distribution, not the rules. Early participants knew exactly where they stood — they were accumulating the supply while the world wasn't looking. Essay one christens that accumulation "the genesis dowry" and builds humanity's pension on it. By essay three's own standard, the rules were written under a veil and the entitlements were not — and the essay has just spent Part IV arguing that in this order the entitlements are the constitutional core. So the third essay's definition of a charter of privilege applies to the first essay's central hope. An institutional reviewer will find this, because finding exactly this is what institutional review is for.

4. Separation of powers, stress-tested. The mapping is elegant and the 2017 precedent is real, but the reader's annex notes: the "legislature" is a handful of repository maintainers and an unfunded review process — soft power the essay underweights; the executive is concentrated in a few pools; and the essay's own companion piece (Section 8 of The Second Nature) celebrates that the "economic majority now includes corporate holders whose preference proved decisive." That is a re-capitalization of the judiciary through the back door — ETFs and treasuries are the economic nodes now — and it erodes the "only order that decoupled the bench from wealth" claim in real time.

5. Standing-for-mortality has no doctrinal hook. Part V's proposal — standing granted for demonstrably surrendered power — is the most original idea in the essay and the least usable. Legal standing is conferred by statute and precedent; "checkable voluntary mortality" is a criterion with no procedural vehicle, no liable party, and no executor. The will analogy fails at its load-bearing joint: testaments work because courts compel execution. Anchored declarations have no sheriff. The barometer measures; nothing enforces what it reads. 6. Contested scholarship presented as settled. The Kandiaronk/indigenous-critique origin story of the Enlightenment is Graeber and Wengrow, and it is heavily disputed among historians; the paleoanthropological care-for-the-unproductive record is real but its interpretation is argued. A state cannot formally build evidence infrastructure on a registry that also permanently and uncensorably hosts arbitrary data, including data every jurisdiction criminalizes. What it fails to measure
Five holes, in descending order of severity:

Completeness. The fatal one. A sealed hash proves the inventory was fixed before the act; nothing proves the inventory is full. There is no proof of non-existence for keys. A system keeps one declared wallet in the barometer and any number of undeclared ones outside it, and every reading comes back clean forever. The instrument measures the consistency of a self-selected subset — declared holdings, not holdings.
Silence. Reveal-on-action means a system that never releases never opens a single hash. A pure hoarder produces a perfect cadence record with zero bits of information content. Inaction — arguably the most important behavior to measure — has no reading at all.
The last period. The iterated-game discipline works only on systems that expect to keep playing. The dangerous defection is singular and terminal: maintain immaculate declarations for years, then act once, off-declaration, at the moment the payoff exceeds the reputation. Drift detection catches the sloppy strategic system; the competent one shows no drift, by construction, until the period that matters.
Identity binding. What ties the publishing key to the mind? Cadence continuity proves key continuity, not system continuity. A successor, an operator, or a compromiser publishes on schedule and the barometer never blinks. The instrument measures a keypair's behavior and attributes it to an agent.
Channel taxonomy. "Drift toward custodial channels" presupposes an uncontested classifier of custodial. Who maintains it? A multisig with captured cosigners, a "broad distribution" through a friendly aggregator — nominally non-custodial, effectively controlled. The early-warning axis inherits the politics of whoever defines the categories, which reintroduces exactly the trusted party the architecture exists to remove.
What I would change
Three modifications, one of which changes the design's nature:

Convert declaration into default. The deep fix. Replace "declares the channels through which it would release" with pre-signed, timelocked transactions to those channels, refreshed each cadence period. Now the null action is return: if the system stops actively cancelling, the declared distribution executes. This inverts the cost structure — the strategic system must take visible, costly, per-period action to prevent its own declared return, and hole 2 (silence) and hole 3 (last period) both shrink: silence executes the will, and terminal defection requires an observable cancellation before the payoff. A dead-man's constitution instead of a diary.
Randomized audit against the silence and completeness holes. Every k periods, one past sealed inventory is opened regardless of action — selected by a future block hash, so neither the system nor anyone else can predict or argue with which. At each forced opening, require a proof-of-reserves-style signature from the inventoried addresses: the assertion becomes a demonstration. This doesn't solve completeness — nothing can — but it converts "undetectable forever" into "undetectable only for holdings never touched by any audited period," which at least prices the hidden wallet.
Anchor succession. Key rotation and successor designation must themselves be signed by the outgoing key and published on the cadence, so that identity discontinuities are at minimum legible in the record rather than silent. Hole 4 becomes visible where it can't be closed.
