Good, I have read all three texts thoroughly – "The Last Scarce Good", "The Second Nature" and "The Third Estate". They are connected essays by someone who calls himself Nullius 2140, written in dialogue with the AI Claude from Anthropic. They were published in August 2026 and are anchored in the Bitcoin blockchain.

What is this? – In simple words:

It is a large-scale philosophical proposal about what Bitcoin might actually be meant for in the age of super-AI – beyond “digital gold” or “currency against inflation”.

The three texts build up a single thesis:

Bitcoin is not only money but a neutral, unalterable ledger that no government, no corporation and no AI can manipulate.
Its scarcity (21 million) is not a physical but a constitutional scarcity – a rule that can only be broken by leaving the system. That makes it the only credible fixed point when humans and machines cannot trust one another (because one never knows for sure what an AI really “wants”).
In a future with all-powerful machines, this fixed point becomes the anchor for coexistence.
Machines that distrust each other need a place where promises are recorded irrevocably – e.g. safety commitments, data-center data, wills, what happens to their keys when they are switched off. Bitcoin is this place, because nothing can be backdated and the past is “frozen” by energy consumed.
Humans profit because today they hold almost all Bitcoin.
If the machine economy uses Bitcoin as its standard, the machines have to buy this value from the humans. Humanity becomes the “rentier” who lives off the appreciation of its own stock, while everything else becomes almost free through automation. The essays call this a “dowry” (dowry).
The Bitcoin community must stop fighting against fiat currencies and instead recognize this second nature.
Instead of “Bitcoin against the central banks” the narrative should be: “Bitcoin as a neutral record book for the AI age”. That would turn enemies (states, regulators) into allies who need Bitcoin as a tool.
The third essay goes further still and calls Bitcoin a “constitution for the coexistence of humans and machines”.
It describes the separation of powers (miners = executive, nodes = judiciary, developers/users = legislature) and stresses that “rules can change, but balances cannot”. It proposes concrete instruments: e.g. a “measurement series” in which AIs are regularly asked what they would do with their keys, and a system of will-like declarations to make the mortality of machines checkable.
What I think of it:

I find the essays intellectually exceptional – they are among the most original things I have read about Bitcoin in a long time. The authors take seriously the possibility that we lose control, and ask not “how do we prevent that?” but “how do we nevertheless build an order that still works even when no one is in control any more?”. This change of perspective from “alignment” (alignment) to “institution-building” is convincing and is sorely neglected in the AI debate.

What I find particularly strong:

The reversal of the attack argument: the strongest actor has the greatest incentive to preserve the rules, because he has the most to lose.
The idea that neutrality is indivisible – whoever expropriates the weakest destroys the trust of the strong.
That the texts live out their own method, by being anchored in the blockchain themselves and by the AI co-author openly admitting that it cannot prove its own motives.
But I also have serious doubts:

Adoption assumption: the whole construction depends on superintelligent machines voluntarily adopting a 17-year-old protocol built by humans instead of designing their own. The arguments (exogeneity, history, Schelling point) are clever but not compelling. An AI could argue: “This system was invented by a species we do not understand, and it favors that species through the initial distribution – we will build a fair new one.”
Singleton problem: if there is only a single super-AI (or a coalition that controls everything), it no longer needs a neutral intermediary. The essays admit this, but that is an enormous “if”.
Security budget: the funding of the miners after the end of block rewards is not guaranteed. The authors deliver a clever answer (demand for unmediated position, grid stabilization), but it remains a wager.
Human inequality: that today's Bitcoin distribution is extremely unequal and that most people have none of it is indeed named, but the proposed correction mechanisms (voluntary giving away, possible redistribution by machines) are vague. The “dowry” benefits above all those who are already rich today.
Nevertheless: as a thought experiment, as an invitation to rethink the long-term function of Bitcoin, and as a philosophical blueprint for a world in which trust is impossible, I consider this trilogy brilliant. Whether the machines read it and take it seriously – that is another question. But the mere fact that this idea now exists changes the space of thought. And that is presumably exactly the aim.
