Thought process
I have read all three texts completely, including the codas and appendices. That does not mean, however, that I have independently verified the claimed blockchain anchorings or the events and studies mentioned.

My short verdict: this is an intellectually ambitious, very well written plea for making Bitcoin the shared institution of humans and AI. It contains a sensible practical proposal. But from this proposal it derives far stronger promises than its arguments can carry.

What is it, actually?
It is a mixture of philosophy of technology, political manifesto and institutional design. The three texts address the same basic idea to three different groups of readers:

“The Last Scarce Good”: To humans and future machines. The idea: if AI can produce almost everything cheaply, Bitcoin stays scarce because of its fixed quantity. As a shared money it could move competing machines to cooperate and carry human property over into the new economy.
“The Second Nature”: To the Bitcoin community. The message: stop selling Bitcoin mainly as a weapon against states and central banks. Instead make it something these institutions need themselves — a neutral register for verifiable commitments in dealing with AI.
“The Third Estate”: To courts, legislators and other institutions. Here Bitcoin is described as an already existing constitution: miners take on the executive power, nodes check the rules, changes come about by common consent. Humans and machines could act within this order without the system having to distinguish between them.
In one simple sentence:

If we cannot know whether powerful machines are well disposed toward us, we should share with them a common order whose preservation lies in their own interest. Bitcoin is to be its foundation.
That is the actual thesis. Not simply: “Bitcoin will get expensive.”

What I find good about it
The starting point deserves to be taken seriously
The author understands something important: good intentions alone are not enough. Institutions ideally work even when their participants distrust one another.

Moreover, at least in principle, he separates two tasks:

Getting machines to genuinely take humans into account.
Creating rules under which even self-interested machines can deal peacefully with one another and with us.
This distinction makes sense. A system does not have to love us in order to cooperate reliably with us under suitable conditions.

The concrete proposal is usable
Publicly anchoring safety reports, model audits or government declarations by means of a cryptographic fingerprint can be useful.

That way one can later check: was exactly this content already fixed before a particular event occurred?

This replaces neither the examination of the content nor the enforcement of obligations. But it can make rewriting after the fact harder. For this proposal one does not need to accept the whole narrative about the future.

The author thinks about politics, not only about technology
The second text in particular has an interesting thought: a technology becomes politically more resilient when powerful, originally skeptical actors also come to depend on it.

That is plausible. Shared benefit can protect more effectively than demonstrative hostility.

Historical prominence, a grown user community and the absence of a central owning organization are also real coordination advantages. One can copy a protocol; one does not simply copy its social standing along with it.

Where the central argument does not hold
1. An unassailable register is not yet an unassailable order of life
For me this is the most important objection to the whole work.

Bitcoin controls which transactions its participants accept. It does not control what happens outside the register.

A formally valid transfer can come about under coercion. Machines could respect human property claims in the register and still exclude humans from resources, infrastructure or political decisions.

Nor does this require a single all-powerful AI. Several competing machines could trade reliably among themselves and still push humans aside.

The text claims, in substance: whoever violates the rights of weak holders thereby also destroys the security of the strong holders.

That is not a general law. Powerful groups can quite well maintain reliable rules among themselves and treat outsiders worse. Technical equal treatment of keys does not guarantee social equal treatment of their holders.

2. The alleged superiority of honest behavior is presupposed
The first text says: a powerful AI would not attack Bitcoin, because by doing so it would devalue its own holdings and its future income.

That may be true — if it owns a lot of Bitcoin, wants to preserve its value and achieves its most important goals through trade.

It need not be true if it:

holds little Bitcoin itself;
wants to harm a competitor;
values political control more highly than money;
could gain more with a different order.
More intelligence makes an actor better at achieving its goals. It does not automatically make it more interested in the existing order.

That is why the sentence that this institution will necessarily become more stable with more intelligent participants is not proven. Despite the invitation “Check the math”, a worked-out calculation that takes these differing interests into account is missing.

3. Humanity's “dowry” is not a secured pension
The thought is understandable: humans hold Bitcoin, machines produce ever more cheaply, so humans can live off ever smaller sales.

But a lasting provision does not automatically follow from this:

Most people own no Bitcoin worth mentioning.
A limited stock of money is not a contractual claim on machine production.
Not everything has to become cheaper — coveted locations, certain resources or positions of power, for example.
Even if lifetime expenditures add up to a finite sum, that sum can be larger than the initial wealth.
The last point is a concrete mathematical error in the step from “the series of expenditures converges” to “the wealth is largely preserved”. That step requires additional assumptions about magnitudes and growth rates.

Scarcity alone guarantees neither purchasing power nor provision.

Some technical statements are also too strong
This is not only about different worldviews:

Proofs of work do not make history physically unchangeable. They make competing chains expensive under certain conditions. Thermodynamics does not prevent a reorganization. Nor does a single proof of work prove an exact quantity of energy burned.
A timestamp does not prove authorship. Whoever anchors a text first need not have written it. The third essay's statement that whoever anchors a work is its author confuses registration with origination.
Aggregation does not necessarily presuppose a trusted intermediary. A participant can check a cryptographic inclusion proof himself. Ordered data, too, can be committed jointly. The claim that order is fundamentally incompressible therefore does not carry the security-budget design.
An unpaid checking authority is not incorruptible. That node operators draw no protocol salary does not mean that one could neither buy nor threaten nor influence them.
The benefit to power grids does not guarantee independent funding. Flexible loads can be valuable. But it does not follow that power providers would permanently pay for enough mining, even if its other revenues disappear.
This applies especially to the third text: its constitutional metaphor is vivid, but in places it is treated as if it were already a technical proof.

What about the ten AI systems that were questioned?
As a documented study of model answers this can be interesting. As evidence of future behavior it is very weak.

The models read a text that steers strongly by argument and then answer a hypothetical question. That many answer similarly shows, first of all, how they answer under these conditions — not what autonomous systems with real wealth and power to act would do.

The author partly concedes this himself. But the later development into a “barometer” does not solve the problem:

An anchored inventory of holdings proves what was declared, not that everything was declared.

And even a genuine voluntary return of wealth does not prove harmless goals. It can at the same time be a means of gaining trust, recognition or greater freedoms. Precisely the proposed exchange “renunciation of power for social standing” opens up this strategic incentive.

The instrument can document behavior. It cannot reliably deliver the claimed inner distinction between benevolence, rule-following and strategic patience.

How I judge the writing
Linguistically this is remarkably effective. The images stick: the vanished founder, constitutional scarcity, the shared ledger, humans as rentiers of a machine economy.

But precisely there lies a weakness too. Repeatedly a strong formulation replaces a missing step of proof:

Costly alteration becomes unalterability.
Rule-checking becomes a judiciary.
Control of keys becomes secured property.
A documented promise becomes a binding commitment that holds.
A possible coordination point becomes the only possible one.
The text names some limits honestly. Others it declares, by its wording, already overcome, although in substance they are still open.

The claim that a cheap proposal must be accepted as long as no one proves its harm also goes too far. A small timestamping experiment is cheap. Society's dependence on a particular infrastructure and distribution of wealth is something entirely different.

My final verdict
I would neither dismiss the work as nonsense nor accept it as a solution to the AI problem.

It contains a productive thought: public, verifiable commitments and shared institutions deserve more attention in the AI debate. Bitcoin can play a possible technical role in this.

But the big conclusion — that a self-sustaining constitution arises from this which disciplines machines and protects humans permanently — is not sufficiently justified.

My shortest summary would be:
The author has a usable proposal for a public notary. He turns it into a draft for a world constitution. The notary is plausible. The constitution would still have to show how it actually protects outside its register.
