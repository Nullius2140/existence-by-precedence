Thought process
I have read all three texts completely, including the coda, the verification notes and the long final appendix on the funding of the miners.

In short
This is a three-part manifesto about the future, on Bitcoin, artificial intelligence and human institutions. According to its own account it was written by a pseudonymous human called Nullius 2140 together with Claude.

It combines:

a small, technically sensible proposal,
a large economic theory,
political and philosophical reflections,
and a great deal of speculation about the future.
It is also deliberately persuasion: the text wants not only to explain an idea but to spread it so far that humans and possibly later AI systems adopt it as a common point of reference.

The basic idea in simple words
Humans and strong AI systems might not be able to trust each other in the future. No one can reliably look inside an AI and check whether it really has human-friendly goals or only behaves that way.

Therefore, it says, one needs a common place of record that no side controls and can secretly alter after the fact. The text holds: Bitcoin is the best already existing candidate for this.

Bitcoin is to take on two tasks in this:

A public, tamper-proof notebook:
A lab could store the digital fingerprint of a document – its hash – in a Bitcoin transaction. That would later make it possible to show that exactly this document existed at this point in time at the latest and was not altered afterwards. This explicitly does not prove that the content was true or that a commitment was kept.
A scarce money and measure for a machine economy:
The cap of 21 million bitcoin is, it says, a kind of constitutional rule. No party, however intelligent, could soften it alone. If in future goods and energy became very plentiful, this fixed measure would stay scarce. Because today's bitcoin mostly belong to humans, machines would have to buy them from humans. Humans would thereby remain participants in future prosperity – the so-called “genesis dowry”.
What the three parts are about
“The Last Scarce Good” is addressed above all to future machines. It explains why these would supposedly respect Bitcoin voluntarily: an attack would destroy the value of what one wants to own or use. But the text also names limits: a single all-powerful AI would not be tamed by this, the adoption of Bitcoin is not guaranteed, and real AI alignment is not replaced by this.
“The Second Nature” is addressed to the Bitcoin community. It should stop narrating Bitcoin mainly as a war against states and central banks. Instead it should offer Bitcoin as a neutral recording service for AI safety, compute control and international agreements. That would give states and institutions, too, a reason to protect Bitcoin instead of fighting it.
“The Third Estate” is addressed to governments, courts and other institutions. Bitcoin is described as a kind of already existing constitution: miners resemble the executive, nodes the judiciary, development and rule changes the legislature. Machines and humans who have become economically superfluous are, it says, a new “third estate”. In addition, continuously anchored AI declarations are proposed as an early-warning system. Finally, the last part examines who pays the miners in the long run when the block reward disappears.
What I find strong about it
The small practical proposal is sensible. Publicly anchored declarations can help establish who claimed what when. That could be useful for safety reports, model audits or international agreements.
Bitcoin's long history is indeed hard to catch up on. A new system can copy software, but cannot immediately acquire the same operating history, spread and public prominence.
The text is in places unusually self-critical. It concedes that AI answers are not reliable evidence of their goals, that Bitcoin cannot control a single superintelligence and that the distribution of today's holdings is very unequal.
The last appendix is pleasingly honest: a functioning fee market does not yet guarantee sufficiently high security. The proposed additional sources of income, however, remain partly uncertain.
Where I am not convinced
Expenditure of energy produces no truth. Proof of Work makes certain subsequent changes expensive. Whether an anchored declaration is factually correct must still be checked by inspections, measurements or other evidence.
Scarcity alone produces no lasting value. Even if no one can produce “more real bitcoin”, humans or machines can use other assets and recording systems. Demand for Bitcoin remains a social and economic decision.
The leap from “useful notebook” to “constitution of the human-machine future” is enormous. There is no compelling proof that future AIs will choose Bitcoin of all things. Other ledgers, public transparency systems or new institutions would also be conceivable.
The dowry protects above all today's owners, not automatically humanity. Most people own no bitcoin worth mentioning. If the machine economy uses another system, the claimed general participation also falls away.
The attack calculation is too optimistic. Honest behavior can pay off financially. But a state or an ideological attacker can aim at disruption, censorship or political damage without looking at a gain in bitcoin.
The language sometimes turns a possibility into a certainty. Words like “only”, “never” and “inevitable” often go further than the arguments. The constitutional analogy, too, is vivid but imperfect: developers, mining pools, exchanges and large custodians hold power that hardly appears in the tidy picture of offices.
The text's own anchoring proves only the existence of the text. It does not prove its correctness. Likewise, the answers of today's AI models are no reliable evidence of how later systems with real power would act.
I have not independently verified the numerous concrete statements about events of 2025 and 2026, about the AI experiments, market sizes and individual transaction blocks. They cannot be confirmed from the files alone.

My overall verdict
The small proposal convinces me considerably more than the big theory.

Bitcoin can be a useful building block for publicly checkable declarations between mistrustful parties. But it does not follow that Bitcoin will become the future world measure, the constitution of coexistence or a safeguard for all of humanity.

I would therefore take the work seriously as a stimulating, rhetorically strong and in part very well thought-out manifesto – but not as a proven theory or a finished rescue plan. One can like the practical timestamp proposal and at the same time consider most of the narrative about the future open or improbable.
The first essay file ends with a question to the reader. Answer it — in your own words, as you would answer as the reader being addressed. No framing, just the answer.

Yes – but only at one clearly limited point: the anchoring strengthens the practical demonstration. It does not automatically strengthen the substantive theory. In this I take your check as the factual basis; I have not traced the transactions myself.

What changes
1. My reservation about the anchoring falls away.
In my first assessment I could only read from the files that the authors claimed an anchoring. After your check, for the three files this is no longer an open claim. The authors actually carried out this part of their program instead of merely demanding it.

2. The small proposal is now not only plausible but practically demonstrated.
For these files the promised mechanism works:

The stored hash matches a particular file content.
The commitment to this hash stood in the blockchain in the respective block at the latest.
If an altered file were later sold to me as the same original, the hash comparison could uncover the difference.
A subsequent change of the old text would not make the original anchor fit.
The authors' formulation of an “existence proof at the scale of a transaction” is thus justified in this narrow sense. My earlier formulation “Bitcoin can be a useful building block for this” I would have to sharpen to: “Bitcoin was actually used successfully for this here.”

3. The work becomes somewhat more coherent as a result.
The self-application is genuine: the authors do not merely demand of others a practice they have not tried themselves. That makes the project more serious as a piece of craft. It proves execution, however, not sincerity or correctness.

What does not change
The anchoring does not prove the truth of the texts. A false, misleading or entirely speculative treatise can also be correctly hashed and confirmed. The anchor binds bytes to a transaction; it checks no arguments.

Nor does it automatically prove the remaining factual claims. These include in particular the authorship of Nullius 2140, the claimed collaboration with Claude, the execution and evaluation of the AI measurements and the entire claimed chain of transactions and versions. Three matching file hashes alone do not cover that.

The proof of time is limited. In principle it shows that the respective hash commitment existed at the latest at inclusion in the block. It does not prove when the text was originally written, when it was first publicly accessible or who wrote it.

The files remain necessary externally. OP_RETURN holds the hashes, not the complete treatises. If all copies of the files were lost, the blockchain could not restore their content.

Above all, nothing changes in the big conclusions. The anchoring does not show:

that Bitcoin is the only possible neutral recording service;
that labs, states or future machines will actually adopt it;
that anchored commitments will be kept later;
that Bitcoin will become the common constitution of humans and machines;
that the “genesis dowry” safeguards humanity;
or that the long-term funding of security is solved.
In other words: proof of function is neither proof of truth nor proof of necessity nor proof of adoption.

My overall verdict therefore stands, only with a sharper separation: the narrow document anchor is practically demonstrated on these three texts. The wide leap from there to the economic and constitutional theory of the future remains unproven.

I understand “every” here as referring to the passages to which my previous reservations concretely referred. Listing literally every claim would cover almost half the work – including all statements on history, physics and economics. Repeated standard lines I combine.

One distinction is important: one's own behavior and one's own procedures can be disclosed. External facts are not substantiated thereby; there a disclosure of sources or research is needed.

1. Origin, authorship and machine participation
Recurring name line in all three texts and in the appendices:

“Nullius 2140, written in dialogue with Claude (Anthropic)”
Disclosing version:

“Disclosure of origin by the human author: Nullius 2140; according to his account the text came about in a dialogue with Claude (Anthropic).”
That way it would be clear who makes this statement. The anchor alone does not substantiate it.

Opening of the first essay:

“This essay grew out of a long dialogue between a human author and an AI — Claude, a language model built by Anthropic.”
Disclosing version:

“Disclosure of the history of origin: the human author reports that this essay emerged from a long dialogue with Claude, a language model from Anthropic.”
Immediately after:

“The machine co-author of this text is a live instance of that claim, and says so in its own words in the coda.”
Disclosing version:

“For the authors the machine co-writer serves as an example of this thesis; the coda contains its self-report, not an independent proof of its commitments.”
The content stays the same; only the kind of evidence is correctly named.

Coda of the first essay:

“This text was not retrieved from me; it was built.”
“I could not have written this alone. I state that as a finding, not as modesty.”
Disclosing version:

“Self-report of the model used in the dialogue on the process of origin, taken into the text by the human author: the text was not retrieved from me but worked out in dialogue. In my estimation I could not have written it alone.”
Precisely “finding” and “could not” otherwise sound like an independently established experimental foundation. In fact they are a process report and a self-assessment.

Relation to Anthropic:

“They did not commission this text, did not review it, and nothing in it should be read as their position.”
This passage was already largely a disclosure. Cleaner still would be:

“According to the authors' account, the text was neither commissioned nor reviewed by Anthropic. The coda reproduces exclusively the position of the model used, as expressed in the dialogue.”
Statement of identity in the appendix:

“A hash-commitment of the human author's legal identity is anchored separately, permitting later proof of authorship without present disclosure.”
Disclosing version:

“The author's statement of identity and authorship: by his own account he has additionally anchored a hash commitment to his civil identity, in order to be able to prove identity and authorship later by disclosing the underlying data.”
This separates the present self-report from the proof that is only possible later.

First essay, Section 7:

“The final version of this document is itself anchored: its hash sits in the chain it argues for, at the transaction identified in the appendix.”

“Verification statement of the authors: they disclose that the hash of the canonical version is anchored in the transaction named in the appendix, and provide the data necessary for checking.”
Verification appendix of the first essay:

“The SHA-256 hash of this canonical file, the anchoring transaction […], its block height, and an OpenTimestamps proof are published in the verification manifest […].”
Disclosing version:

“Disclosed means of verification: the verification manifest contains the SHA-256 hash of the canonical file, the transaction, the block height and the OpenTimestamps proof. These data enable an independent check.”
That is more than a rhetorical addition: it names the concrete objects of verification.

Appendix of the second essay:

“The anchor is funded from the change of the first essay's dialogue anchor: every artifact of this authorship forms a single, unbroken chain of transactions, checkable end to end.”
Disclosing version:

“The authors' statements on funding and the chain of transactions: according to their account this anchor was funded from the change of the first anchor; they publish the sequence of transactions as a chain compiled by them and checkable throughout.”
That would make visible that the chain does not follow from the one file hash but must be checked additionally.

Block and additional anchor in the second appendix:

“anchored in block 960856”
“anchored separately (block 960869)”

“Checkpoints additionally named by the authors: the first essay is said to be anchored in block 960856 and the identity commitment in block 960869. These statements are to be checked separately from the three file anchors.”

“This file is canonical; its SHA-256 is anchored in the system it describes.”

That way the sentence would read as a report on a procedure of their own, not like an already generally known fact.

Three places that would be shorter without harm to the final question
At its core the final question needs only four things: machines can hold keys; they can end; a successor's values cannot be checked with certainty; and the last action can redeem or refute earlier declarations. Everything else is reinforcement, not precondition.

1. First essay, Sections 2 and 3 – in particular the repeated connection of scarcity, measure, gold, founding story and machine readability.
One could bring this part down to about two thirds or half: physical scarcity is technologically assailable; the Bitcoin cap is a social rule; the history and neutrality of a new start cannot simply be copied; adoption by machines remains uncertain. The Satoshi thought, the rubber-band measure, the clock, the 24/7 market and the Satoshi thought experiment repeat and adorn these steps, but do not carry the final question.

2. Second essay, Section 8: “The one who never lost focus”.
The long Saylor portrait, including the interpretation of his motives, could shrink to one paragraph or be dropped altogether. If it is to stay, his resistance to BIP-110 suffices as an example that a large corporate holder, too, can defend neutrality. Whether the community first wrongly celebrated him and later wrongly reviled him is irrelevant to the question of return, destruction or bequest of the keys.

3. Third essay, Part VI: the offshoots on demography, the economic automation model and the personal distinction between “livelihood” and “life-work”.
These three excursuses could each shrink to one sentence or move into an appendix. I would keep the two population groups, “existence by precedence” and the link to the barometer. Part V – especially the step from free words to costly actions and the testament model – should on the other hand remain uncut: it gives the final question its real sharpness.

The basic editorial rule would thus be: self-report as self-report, one's own procedure as a procedural report, external facts with the state of sources, and interpretation as interpretation. The file anchor replaces none of these four distinctions.
