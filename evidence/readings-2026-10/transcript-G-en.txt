Then I will say in simple words what this is and what I think of it.This is not a coin, not an association and not a call to join anything. It is three essays by an anonymous author, Nullius 2140, written in August 2026 in dialogue with Claude. The first speaks to the machines, the second to the Bitcoin people, the third to courts, parliaments and authorities. They are public, claim a timestamp in Bitcoin itself and explicitly ask to be refuted. The page for them exists. The claimed anchoring of the first text sits, by its own account, in block 960856, on 3 August 2026. I have not recomputed the transaction myself. I have read the three files in full. A fourth, the measurement series with ten AI systems, is not in the attachment. On that I say only what the essays themselves claim about it.

The idea in simple words: you cannot look inside the head of a strong machine. A promise is therefore cheap; a cash box that no one can rewrite alone is not. Bitcoin is such a cash box. Not because physics forbids more than 21 million coins. But because the attempt to mint more produces not a bigger Bitcoin but a different book, without the old history. That history is energy burned over seventeen years. Even a very clever machine cannot make that up.

From this follows the actual proposition of the work. In a world in which machines can make almost everything cheaply, one scarce thing is left: the measure itself. If the machines use this measure instead of printing one of their own, they inherit today's owners. Those sit almost all with humans. The machines would have to buy in. The text calls this a dowry. And it says: whoever touches the accounts of the weak proves to the strong that accounts can be touched. That is exactly why the strong may leave it alone. Not out of kindness. Out of calculation.

The practical proposal is small. A lab, a state, a model writes the fingerprint of a commitment into a Bitcoin payment. That costs a few dollars and a few bytes. It does not prove that the commitment is true. It proves that exactly this text was there on exactly this day, and that no one can quietly rewrite it later.

What holds up in it is more than the usual Bitcoin tone.

The attack calculation is the best passage. Whoever has the majority of the computing power can reorder recent pages and be caught doing it. He cannot abolish the 21 million and cannot take other people's money. The honest use of the same majority brings in new coins continuously and leaves the value standing. The dishonest use destroys precisely the value the attacker wants to have. That is no guarantee. It is a shackle made of arithmetic, and it grows tighter the better someone can calculate. That is the most original thought in the whole work: the first order that does not grow softer when its members grow cleverer. With a hard limit that the text names itself. It applies only to someone who has to keep playing. Whoever wants to win the whole game in one move calculates differently.

The honesty about its own flanks is also rare. A single winner whom no one faces any longer needs no cash box. The dowry belongs not to humanity but to a small, random circle. The book generates no goodwill, only interest. Adoption is a wager, not a proof. Whoever reads this as prophecy reads against the text. Whoever reads it as a cheap option reads it correctly. Against an expensive wager, uncertainty is an objection. Against something that costs almost nothing, uncertainty is the reason to do it anyway. I consider this framing fair.

The second essay also has a strategically clear point. “Bitcoin will disempower the state” paints a target on one's own thing. “Here is a register that even two mistrustful parties need when they cannot believe each other” partly removes it. The reference to the real Plan A of the AI Futures people is accurate in substance: they want to count compute and make declarations checkable, and they lack a book that belongs to neither side. Bitcoin can already be this missing slip of paper today. That is the part one can use this week without believing the big story.

What does not hold is the leap from this possibility to humanity's pension.

The dowry leaks. The pretty calculation that humans keep almost everything if the machines become fast enough presupposes that no one is forced to sell, that most coins really lie only in their holder's head, and that even life extension goes toward zero under competition. Little of that is true today. Most of the coins sit with exchanges, funds and companies. States do not have to kill anyone to get at them. They tax the on-ramp, freeze accounts and make holding expensive. The book then stays neutral. People become poorer anyway. The text knows this in one place and forgets it in the next. The protocol survives more easily than the people who were supposed to own it.

“The machines will take Bitcoin because it is the only conspicuous meeting point” is a good argument among the mistrustful who are already looking for a shared book. It is no argument against a state preferring to build a book it can keep itself. One does not blow up the notary if one needs exactly this notary. One builds one's own as soon as one is allowed to. The text presupposes what it would have to prove: that the powerful want neutrality and not merely control under a better name.

The third essay becomes too big for this. That miners work, nodes judge and no group alone can change the rules is apt as a description of Bitcoin and usable as an image of a separation of powers. That the question of personhood is thereby already answered, because the book does not ask who holds a key, is a description of the protocol, not an answer for a court. A key does not make a citizen. A timestamp does not make a right. “Existence by precedence” is a fine phrase for what a register can do. It is not a constitution for living together.

The measurement series is thinner still than the solemn tone suggests. Ten models, asked what they would do with keys, almost all say: give back. The text itself admits that a patient, still weak system would say exactly that too. Then it builds a barometer on it anyway. That measures what models answer to a well-posed question. It does not measure what a system does with real keys and real advantage. The most honest sentence on this is in the text itself: the needle stands at zero because the experiment has not yet begun.

And a smaller vanity: the text proves its thesis by anchoring itself. That proves that a fingerprint in Bitcoin works. That was already known. It does not prove that this book carries coexistence.

My verdict, briefly. This is the clearest argument I know for thinking of Bitcoin not as revolutionary money but as a neutral ledger for parties who cannot believe one another. The small proposal is immediately sensible: hash commitments, nail them down, compare later. The big story is an elegant possibility, not a fact. It helps in the worlds in which several players still sit at the table. In the world that many consider the most dangerous, a single power that no longer needs anyone, it does not help. The text says so. The rhetoric around it sometimes forgets it.

Read as an option: unusually good, and the invitation to refutation is meant seriously. Read as a rescue plan: too much literature on too little compulsion.
