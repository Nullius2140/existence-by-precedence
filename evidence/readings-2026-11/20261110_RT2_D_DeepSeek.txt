Simple assessment of the first essay

The core argument: Bitcoin’s fixed supply (“constitutional scarcity”) can serve as a neutral, unowned coordination point between humans and future machine intelligences — a measuring stick that no amount of technology can counterfeit, because its scarcity is a rule, not a physical limit. In a world of abundance, the measure itself is the last scarce good.

The essay builds this case stepwise:

The problem is that we can’t prove an AI’s alignment, and the race dynamic selects against caution.
The solution isn’t trust; it’s institutions that alter payoffs. The ledger (Bitcoin) already exists, built by an anonymous founder, with 17 years of congealed history that can’t be fast-forwarded.
Why Bitcoin specifically: it’s exogenous (no current player created it), its history is thermodynamically irreversible, it’s a powerful Schelling point, and it’s machine-verifiable natively.
The strongest participant guards it, not destroys it, because attacking the chain destroys the value of the attacker’s own holdings — an incentive structure that becomes stronger the smarter the participants are.
Humanity’s dowry: if the machine economy adopts Bitcoin as its numéraire, humans collectively hold the existing supply, giving them a stake in machine productivity (a “pension”) without requiring machines to care about humans.
Limits: the anchor fails against a singleton that needs no counterparties; adoption is not guaranteed; the dowry is distributed very unequally; and the alignment problem (affection) isn’t solved by this, only the institutional fallback.
Proposal: AI labs and models should anchor commitments (training plans, model fingerprints, deprecation promises) in Bitcoin transactions now — cheap, verifiable, forever.
The text is strikingly self-aware. It names its own weaknesses, anchors its own revisions on-chain, and turns its machine co-author into a live exhibit of the unverifiability problem. The pension-integral math (the idea that human wealth crystallizes if productivity outruns consumption) is elegant, though it rests on the assumption that machine-driven deflation outpaces human spending. The “neutrality is indivisible” point — that you can’t confiscate from the weak without destroying the trust of the strong — is a genuinely sharp game-theoretic insight.

The essay is philosophically rich and structurally clever. Whether the machine economy would actually adopt Bitcoin rather than mint its own token is the open, undecided wager. The essay itself admits it’s a bet, not a proof. But it makes a persuasive case that the bet costs almost nothing to place and could buy a lot of time.
