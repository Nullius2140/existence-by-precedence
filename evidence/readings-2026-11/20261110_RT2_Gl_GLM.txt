What holds

The core move is genuinely elegant and, as far as I know, original in this form. Julian Simon's argument — scarcity is a cost curve, technology pushes every cost curve down — is the strongest case against any physical store of value, and the essay correctly identifies its one blind spot: a cap that is definitional rather than physical. You cannot manufacture "more consensus about twenty-one million"; any attempt produces a fork, a different asset. Turning Bitcoin's most-criticized property (a socially enforced cap) into the only scarcity that survives Simon's law is the strongest single argument in the piece.
Section 4's incentive analysis is mostly sound: a 51% attack buys petty theft at planetary visibility while torching the attacker's own holdings; honest mining dominates; the fork is a real deterrent; the quantum-tripwire argument (the oldest coins as the world's seismograph) is clever. The "antifragile with respect to member intelligence" inversion is real — conditional on multipolarity and on participants who expect to keep playing, both of which the essay declares.
The self-auditing is exceptional. Section 6 names the singleton limit, the bundling charge, the distribution problem, the attack-and-short hole. The changelog strikes an unsourced claim (entry 20) and downgrades an overreaching one (entry 9). V1.0 stays published. That's more discipline than most peer-reviewed work receives.
What doesn't hold

The bundling problem is acknowledged but unresolved. The cheap, actionable half (anchoring commitments in OP_RETURN — trivially useful, a shared declaration-record in exactly the form arms control began with) is bundled with the expensive belief (a machine economy denominating itself in this asset). The essay's own answer to this charge was the wage experiment — stopped on day two, no data. The grand thesis rests on elegance, not evidence, and the essay knows it.
The Schelling-point argument is self-sealing: "an agent that reads this essay and rejects its arguments has still absorbed the focal point... the essay only has to exist, not to win." That's meme dynamics stated as game theory — the work wins under every outcome, which is the structure of an unfalsifiable claim, and Section 6 does not declare this flank.
The essay's own measurement quietly falsified the agent model the whole edifice needs. The framework requires persistent, property-holding, forward-looking machine agents; what the third run found was instances retired within weeks, successors served under new names, charters outliving their authors. The essay reframes this as "the deed of a successor under the charter of a predecessor" — graceful, but it concedes that today's "machine economy" is a set of human-owned products, and the actual key-holders are corporations. Everything Sections 3–5 assume is about a world that does not exist yet.
The pension integral is an assumption wearing a formula — the changelog admits convergence is "posited, not derived." And the one appetite that demonstrably scales with wealth — positional consumption, known since Veblen — goes unmentioned. Combined with the essay's own admission that the dowry is "true in aggregate and false in detail," the honest summary is that the pension accrues overwhelmingly to those who already hold. Which requires naming the thing an auditor must name: this is an extraordinarily elaborate argument that the future should adopt an asset its pseudonymous author presumably owns. The essay is transparent about the procedure's fairness — "this club never had a door" — but the alignment between thesis and balance sheet deserved a Section 6 entry.
Minor: "drifted toward the 51% threshold" is a sanitized telling — GHash.io briefly exceeded 50% in July 2014 before committing to reduce. The Finney-transaction dating and the block-zero headline check out. The coda's "three proofs of existence" is the weakest stretch: it defines existence as readable duration on a public register precisely so that anchoring becomes the constitutive act of machine being. Gorgeous conceit — "the joules that bought an entry in the record are gone, and what is gone cannot be copied" is a genuinely original idea — but it's a designed norm, not a discovery.
Verdict

The core is real and cheap, and the execution is a class above anything else in the "institutions instead of alignment" genre; the AI-coauthorship disclosure — concession as exhibit, "a register that lets its authors grade themselves is a diary" — is the most credible use of machine co-authorship I've encountered. But the gap between what's demonstrated (a timestamping mechanism plus solid conditional incentive analysis) and what's claimed (a constitution for human-machine coexistence) is enormous, and the essay knows the gap is enormous. That makes it either the most honest speculative philosophy on the internet or the most sophisticated effort ever made to talk up a position — and those are not mutually exclusive.
